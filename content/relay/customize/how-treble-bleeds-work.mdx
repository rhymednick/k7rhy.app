Turn the volume knob down on most guitars and the sound gets darker as well as quieter. A **treble bleed** is a small part, or a pair of parts, on the volume pot that keeps the highs from fading so fast. This page builds the picture one step at a time: what the volume knob actually does, why turning it down loses the highs, what a bleed changes, and how a version called a **reactive bleed** works together with the tone knob.

You don't need to read a schematic to follow along. Every idea starts with a picture, and the words come after it.

## A few words first

These six words come up on every page about guitar wiring. Each one is defined here once, in plain words.

- **Pickup:** the part under the strings that turns their motion into a small electrical signal.
- **Pot:** short for potentiometer, the part behind a volume or tone knob; it has three solder tabs, called lugs, on its back.
- **Wiper:** the sliding contact inside a pot, wired to the middle lug, that moves along the inside of the pot as you turn the knob.
- **Capacitor:** a small part that lets the highs (the bright, sparkly part of the sound) pass easily and holds the lows (the warm, round part) back.
- **Resistor:** a small part that holds back all frequencies by a fixed amount, like a narrow spot in a pipe.
- **Ground:** the shared return wire that every part of the circuit connects to; signal sent to ground is gone from the sound.

Inside the pot is a **track**, a strip of resistive material, with the wiper riding on it. One end of the track is the input end, where the signal from the pickups comes in. The other end goes to ground. The wiper is the output, and it goes on toward the amp.

Seen from the back, with the shaft pointing away and the lugs at the bottom, the left lug is the input end, the middle lug is the wiper and the right lug is the ground end. The diagrams call the ends **CW** and **CCW** (clockwise and counterclockwise) because the wiper reaches the CW end when the knob is turned all the way up and the CCW end when it is turned all the way down.

So the volume pot is an adjustable divider. Move the wiper toward the input end and more of the signal reaches the amp; move it toward the ground end and less does. Turned all the way up, the wiper sits right on the input end and the signal goes straight through.

Here the volume pot is drawn as the part of its track the signal has to push through on its way to the cable. At full volume there is none of it. As you turn down, more of the track sits between the pickup and the cable.

The cable matters here too. A guitar cable acts like a tiny capacitor from the signal to ground. A capacitor lets highs through easily, so this one gives the highs a path to drain away to ground before they reach the amp.

Put the two together and you have a filter. The lows carry on to the amp. The highs drain away through the cable. The more resistance sits ahead of the cable, the more of the highs drain away, which is why turning down from full makes the guitar dull and not just quiet.

This is a volume pot with nothing added. The colored tags name the wires, and the same names appear in every diagram that follows:

- **BUS** (blue) is the main signal wire from the pickups. It lands on the pot's input end.
- **OUT** (red) is the output. It runs from the wiper to the tip of the output jack, which is the connection that carries the signal to the amp.
- **GND** (gray) is ground. It runs from the pot's ground end to the jack's sleeve, the outer connection of the jack.

A treble bleed adds a path around the pot. In this simplest version, a capacitor connects the pot's input end directly to its wiper. Because a capacitor passes highs easily, the highs can skip past the track instead of pushing through it. The lows can't take that shortcut, so they still go through the track and still get turned down.

The result is that turning the volume knob still makes the guitar quieter, but the highs fade less as you go.

Treble bleeds come in three common shapes. All three sit across the volume pot, from the input end to the wiper.

- **Capacitor only.** The highs skip past the pot at every volume setting, so the sound stays bright as you turn down. Because only the highs skip past, this kind can leave the sound thin as you turn down.
- **Capacitor with a parallel resistor.** The resistor sits beside the capacitor and lets some of the whole signal, lows included, skip past too, which keeps the sound from turning thin as you turn down. It also changes how quickly the knob turns the volume down.
- **Capacitor with a series resistor.** The resistor sits in line with the capacitor and trims how much of the highs skip past. Relay Lipstick uses this kind: a 1 nF capacitor and a 150 kΩ resistor in series.

The values of the parts set how strong each one is. They are chosen to suit the pot and tuned by ear.

## Where fixed bleeds disappoint

Every bleed above is a **fixed bleed**. Its parts connect to the volume pot and to nothing else, so it has no way of knowing where the tone knob is.

That matters when you use both knobs together. Rolling the tone knob back is a way of asking for a darker sound. Turn the volume down at the same time, and the fixed bleed keeps doing its job: it keeps passing highs around the volume pot, just as it would at full tone. The tone knob still works, but the bleed is still adding brightness, so rolling the tone back doesn't tame it the way you might expect.

In the illustration above, the orange "Fixed bleed" line stands for every tone setting: the bleed does the same thing wherever the tone knob is.

The reactive bleed starts at the tone pot, so it helps to look at that pot's three lugs first. Seen from the back, with the shaft pointing away:

- The **wiper** rides on the main signal wire from the pickups (BUS).
- The **CCW end** goes through the tone capacitor (22 nF on Relay Lipstick) to ground. The wiper reaches it at tone 0, all the way down.
- The **CW end** is the spare end. The wiper reaches it at full tone. A standard tone circuit leaves one end of the pot unused. Here that spare end feeds the bleed, through a wire tagged REACT.

The reactive bleed takes its signal from that spare CW end instead of straight from BUS. The glow marks the bleed path. Follow it the way the signal goes: from the tone pot's CW end along REACT, through the switch, then through the 1 nF capacitor and the 150 kΩ resistor to the volume pot's wiper.

At full tone, the tone wiper sits right on the CW end, so the bleed gets the same signal it would get from BUS and behaves like an ordinary bleed. As you roll the tone back, the wiper moves away from the CW end, more of the tone pot's track sits between the signal and the bleed, and the signal reaching the bleed fades. It works like the series resistor in the variants above, with that resistance growing as you roll the tone back, so the bleed eases off as the tone comes down.

That is the whole idea. The bleed follows the tone knob instead of ignoring it. In the illustration earlier, the green "Reactive bleed" line shows the reactive bleed with the tone rolled back; at full tone it would sit on the orange line. It is an illustration, not a measured result.

A three-position switch lets one guitar do all three. The switch's common lug connects to the far end of the bleed, and each position sends it somewhere different:

- **Direct:** the bleed connects straight to BUS, like a regular treble bleed.
- **Off:** the bleed connects to nothing, and the tone knob works like any tone knob. The green glow in the middle panel is the tone path. The tone control itself works the same in every mode.
- **Reactive:** the bleed is fed through the tone pot, so rolling the tone back eases the bleed off.

| Mode     | What it does                                                                                   | When you'd want it                                                                               |
| -------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Direct   | A normal treble bleed. The highs fade less as you turn down, wherever the tone knob is.        | You turn the volume down to clean up and want the guitar to stay bright while you do.            |
| Off      | A normal volume and tone circuit with no bleed. The guitar gets darker as you turn down.       | You like the classic darkening as you roll back, or you want the plainest sound to compare with. |
| Reactive | A bleed that follows the tone knob. At full tone it matches Direct; rolled back, it eases off. | You use the volume and tone knobs together and want the darker setting to stay dark.             |

Two settings make the modes easy to check by ear. At full volume all three sound the same, because the wiper sits on the input end and there is nothing for a bleed to skip past. At full tone, Direct and Reactive sound the same, because the tone wiper sits on the CW end and the Reactive feed is the same as BUS.

I have built this circuit and validated it on Relay Lipstick, with the 1 nF and 150 kΩ pair in series as its bleed. Nothing about it is specific to Lipstick, though: it suits any guitar wired with one master volume and one master tone.
